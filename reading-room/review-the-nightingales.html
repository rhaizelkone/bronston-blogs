The premise (spoiler-free)

It's spring 1985 and Lou Miller is weeks from graduating high school when her dad quietly invites his coworker Jim, along with Jim's two very dramatic Pomeranians, to move into their small Oregon home. What he doesn't tell Lou or her mom is that Jim has AIDS. That's it, that's the setup. From there it becomes this quiet, devastating, unexpectedly funny portrait of what happens when one house has to hold a secret, a sickness, and a friendship all at once.

What I loved

The art style is doing SO much work here. It's not paneled like a typical graphic novel, it's sketchbook-meets-journal-meets-scrapbook, and you can feel that choice on every page. Some spreads are loose and rough, like a page torn straight out of someone's notebook mid-thought, and others slow down into these neat, detailed, almost reverent illustrations, and the shift between the two matches exactly where you are emotionally in the story.

One of my favorite touches: Donald Miller gets introduced through an actual drawn operating manual, a full illustrated spread laying him out like a set of instructions, his quirks, his stubbornness, how to handle him. It's such a clever, playful way to hand you a fully formed character in one page, gentle and stubborn all at once, and it fits perfectly with the scrapbook logic of the whole book.

At its core this is a book about a friendship that rearranges the shape of someone's life. Lou doesn't just befriend Jim, she becomes someone different because he was there, and you watch that shift happen in real time on the page. Running underneath it the whole way is grief, the anticipatory kind, the kind you're carrying before the loss even arrives. And threaded through both of those is a strained parent-child relationship, Lou and her mother pulling further apart the closer Lou gets to Jim, rendered with so much restraint. No villain here, just two people who love each other and can't quite find their way back into agreement.

It's a historically sensitive subject and it's handled with so much care, and for a book this short, Wilson builds real depth around Lou and Jim specifically. More than anything, this book is a gut-punch reminder of how contagious ignorance is, and who ends up paying for it. It's about what people lose, and who they lose, in the name of "doing the right thing" by everyone else's standards. And it left me sitting with this thought: basic human decency shouldn't be the exception. It should just be the baseline.

What wasn't for me

My only real complaint is that I wanted more of it. I finished and immediately wanted another hundred pages with these people. But I have to hold that lightly, because this isn't a story that was padded or trimmed for effect. It's based on a real occurrence, pulled from Wilson's own family history, and while he stays mostly out of the frame as a character, you can feel his own experience, he volunteered with AIDS patients in NYC in real life, threaded quietly under Lou's. So the "shortness" I felt wasn't the book failing to go deep. It was just me not wanting to leave.

There's a moment where Lou realizes, through her conversations with Jim, that she's finally getting to know both the confident and the gentle sides of herself, a version of herself she wants to keep for good. As she puts it,

"She loves this man for bringing it out in her."
— the nightingales
the verdict ✧

Should you read it?

Yes — especially if you want your heart quietly wrecked. This is a slim, aching read for anyone who wants an unflinching but tender look at the early AIDS crisis, told through a friendship that rearranges everything it touches. Come for the sketchbook art, stay for the family you'll ache over.

If you know what it's like to battle between what you know is right and the ones you love, if you've ever been alienated for something outside your control, if one friendship has ever changed the whole shape of your life, if you know grief as something you carry rather than something that ends, if you've ever loved a parent or child you couldn't quite reach, then this, my sweet songbird, is for you. 🕊️

FINAL RATING: 4 OUT OF 5 SPINES
